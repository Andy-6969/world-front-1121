# Copperplate Wire

A visual philosophy for small news drawings that must read in a glance and still feel made by hand.

**Ink on paper, nothing else.** Every drawing lives on the page's own paper tone. There are no tinted
boxes behind the pictures, no gradients and no glow. Form comes from one dark ink, laid down the way
an engraver lays it: outlines of a single steady weight, and shade built from parallel hatching,
cross-hatching and stipple. Darkness is earned line by line, so each picture should look as if it
took patient hours at the plate, the work of someone who has drawn ten thousand of them.

**One accent, used like a pin.** A single red marks the fact the reader must not miss: the fire, the
low point, the place on the map. Amber appears only where something burns or shines. If a drawing
holds more than two accents it is shouting; strip it back until the red lands exactly once.

**Specific, never generic.** A drawing shows the thing that happened, not the category it belongs to.
Not "trade" but a laden container ship; not "crime" but a helicopter over a named stretch of coast;
not "awards" but a lit stage. Icons that could illustrate any day are rejected. The test is whether a
reader who missed the news could guess the story from the drawing alone.

**Rhythm through repetition.** Water is a field of horizontal strokes, land a scatter of stipple,
shadow a run of 45-degree lines. The same few marks recur across every picture so the page reads as
one hand's work, a coherent set of plates rather than a pile of clip art. Spacing of the hatching is
calibrated, not guessed, and every edge is placed with painstaking care.

**Words as engraved captions.** Text inside a drawing is rare: a place name or a figure, set small and
tracked, sitting where an engraver would scratch a label into the margin. It never repeats the
headline and never crowds the picture.
